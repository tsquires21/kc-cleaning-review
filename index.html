Cleaning Co. Planner
Nearly there

The plan is better than most pre-launch documents, and the markup says exactly where. The gap is arithmetic: six targets — the $150 minimum, the 40% labor pool, the $150 acquisition ceiling, the 15-customer quota, the $30k expansion gate and the $10k owner draw — sit in six different sections and were never multiplied against each other.

Multiplied out, the plan as written needs about two and a half times the business it thinks it does. Three free changes close nearly all of it.

The two findings worth the whole review

1

Point the sales seat at commercial

Keeping a salesperson is the right instinct — the ticket is the problem, not the role. A residential customer pays a rep once, on ~$300. A commercial contract pays 15–40% of the first month plus a 1–3% residual that keeps paying. One contract carries the contribution of roughly seven residential customers. Residential stays the product; commercial is what can afford a commission. Commercial tab.

2

Guarantee the same cleaner. Nobody else does.

Every franchise in the metro hedges it — "every effort, but illness, vacation and staff changes." It is the top complaint in their own reviews. Solo cleaners on a tight map is what makes an unhedged promise deliverable, which means the operational fix and the marketing hook are the same decision. Edge tab.

The cheapest next 30 days

Roughly $1,000 and a few evenings, before any entity, insurance or software spend. It replaces the three remaining guesses with facts.

A

The document, section by section

Twenty sections, twenty verdicts. Numbers match the grill report's own sections.

1
The ProblemKeep

The do-not-offer list and hazard screening at booking are better than most plans have. They prevent the jobs that lose money and hurt people.

2
The EvidenceNow filled in

Contained three restated assumptions, not evidence. Filled in with real KC numbers below — and the news is mostly good. The 40% pool is within two points of the industry benchmark, and retention is better than assumed.

3
The CustomerKeepOne gap

Biweekly-as-default is right. The free 24-hour re-clean collects $0 but still costs wages, burden and the lost slot — about $125 each time. Price it in rather than absorbing it invisibly.

4
The MoneyChange this

$150 is below KC market for the home described. Local operators publish $0.10–0.20/sq ft, putting a 2,000 sq ft home at $200–400; the national average job is $218. Section 6 says you won't compete on price — this is the section that does. The 40% pool itself is fine (industry norm 41.9%), but only with solo cleaners. See (A.8).

5
DistributionRe-aim

The one paid channel points away from the core product. Recurring residential is priority #1 and comes from referrals and organic — yet the rep is aimed at Realtors, whose work is one-time move-outs, which (A.4) pays no retention bonus on. The comp plan pays the rep worst for the channel it assigns them. The fix is on the Commercial tab.

6
CompetitionKeepTrim

Right instinct, wrong list. Background-screened, insured, checklist, 24-hour guarantee and "same cleaner when practical" are advertised by every franchise here — table stakes, not differentiators. Drop the hedge on that last one and it becomes the only real differentiator in the market. See (E).

7
MoatContradiction

Route density is correctly named first — the one thing a metro-wide franchise structurally cannot copy in one quadrant. Then (A.8) draws a territory from Odessa to Lee's Summit: 33 miles, 42 minutes. The map as drawn defeats the moat as stated.

8
ExecutionTwo fixes

Solo versus two-person crews is the most consequential distinction in the plan, and it is never made. A team doubles paid travel against revenue that doesn't move: solo clears the wage floor comfortably, teams on long routes don't. Second, the backup escalation contact is also one of the cleaners — at 9:15 on a Tuesday she is on a job and you are at work.

9
Time, Travel & PayrollBest section

The strongest part of the document. Identifying compensable between-job travel before hiring anyone is something most operators learn the expensive way. Note it also forces the $149 software tier over the $99 one, since only the higher tier geofences.

10
Sales Operating RulesKeepOne blocker

Commission on collected revenue is exactly right and kills the underbid-to-close spiral. But no base pay appears anywhere, and on commission alone a rep hitting the 15-customer target earns about $1,540/month. That number decides whether the launch test can even run. See (A.16).

11
Employee & Customer ProtectionsKeep

Not deducting damage or re-cleans from W-2 wages is the legally correct answer, and plenty of small operators get it wrong. W-2 over 1099 is also right — with checklists, uniforms and assigned customers, contractor status wouldn't survive a challenge.

12
CommercialNow priced

Correctly flagged as unpriced — while (A.5) sends the rep out to sell it anyway. That was the sequencing error. The bid calculator is now built; it is on the Commercial tab, and it turns this from a blocker into the plan's best opportunity.

13
Ownership & GovernanceSeparate conversation

Separate conversation.

14
ExpansionGate too low

The $30k/month gate produces about $4,500/month to Kaleb against a $10,000 goal — so as written the plan opens a second market at 45% of the objective. The gate belongs at or above the owner-income target.

15
Risk RegisterKeep

Honest and nearly complete. One missing item: what happens to the technology if the person who built it is unavailable.

16
The Kill TestTests the wrong thing

The whole go/no-go rests on one salesperson producing five paid customers in 30 days while earning about $512 in month one under the comp plan as written. That person leaves before the test returns a signal, and you learn about the comp plan rather than the market. Fix the seat before running the test.

17
Three AssumptionsRe-rank

Assumption #2 (does 40% survive real costs) survives the data. Assumption #3 (retention) is better than assumed — about 16 months of average customer life. That leaves acquisition as the only genuine unknown, which is exactly where the 30-day test should point.

18
Strongest Argument AgainstAccurate

"A management and logistics company disguised as a cleaning company. If the systems are weak, Kaleb becomes the system." Right diagnosis — and it deserves a named weekday dispatcher rather than a paragraph.

19
Final VerdictPremature, not wrong

GREEN was called while (A.20) still listed the product's price, commercial pricing, the pay model's legality and insurance costs as unresolved. The direction is right; the light was switched on four sections early.

20
Unresolved Pre-Launch WorkRight list

Items 1, 3, 4 and 5 are the real blockers: KC pricing, true cleaner cost, Missouri travel and overtime sign-off, insurance quotes. Items 1 and 3 are largely answered here; 4 and 5 need one phone call each.

The honest summary

The direction of every finding is well supported — underpricing, the solo-versus-team wage gap, the expansion gate below the goal, the rep's earnings. All published figures or arithmetic on the plan's own numbers.

The magnitudes depend on three things nobody has measured: how long a job really takes, how far apart customers really are, and what a salesperson really costs. Two are answerable in a fortnight with a stopwatch and a map.

(A.12) called commercial pricing the major unresolved item, and (A.5) sent the rep to sell it anyway. This closes that gap: bid from labor hours up, then check the result against market.

Read this before quoting anything

Published janitorial rates per square foot are per MONTH, not per visit. Quoting a per-visit price off a per-month table is how new operators sign a contract they lose money on for a year. Always build from hours; use the per-square-foot figure only as a sanity check afterwards.

Small buildings clean far slower than the book rate. A 2,000 sq ft office runs about 1,400 sq ft/hr against an ISSA book rate of 4,000 — setup, travel between rooms and fixed tasks don't shrink with the building. This single number decides whether a bid is profitable.

Residential is still the business

None of this moves away from recurring residential. It is the product, it is what builds route density, and it is what a buyer eventually pays for. Residential keeps growing the way (A.2) already says it does — referrals, organic, local search — and those channels cost far less than a commission.

What changes is only what the paid seat sells. A residential customer is too small a ticket to fund a commission; a commercial contract is large enough to fund one and pays a residual on top. Same rep, same effort per close, several times the contribution — and it happens to reward exactly the thing commercial buyers care about, since contracts are lost on quality (45%) and communication (28%) far more than price (18%).

E

The one thing nobody claims

(A.6) lists "same cleaner when practical" among the differentiators, hedged. Drop the hedge and it stops being table stakes and becomes the only unclaimed position in the eastern metro.

The finding

Not one operator in this market will promise you the same cleaner every time.

Every national franchise uses a near-identical escape hatch, verified on their own pages: "every effort — but illness, vacation and staff changes." The Cleaning Authority and Molly Maid make no staffing promise at all; they sell consistency of method instead. The one local operator who mentions it, Two Gals & A Broom, does so as hedged small print in an FAQ on SEO town pages — their homepage never raises it.

Their own customers say it is the problem

A different cleaner shows up almost every time.MaidPro — 3.3 stars

I specifically asked for them only. That did not happen.The Cleaning Authority — 2.4 stars

Why the franchises cannot simply fix it

Geographic routing plus part-time staffing makes personnel a dependent variable: the time slot dictates who is available, so the crew falls out of the schedule rather than the customer relationship. Fixing it would mean rebuilding how they route and how they staff, in every franchise, at once.

A tight-footprint operator running solo cleaners has the opposite constraint — the route is short enough that one person can own it. Which means the operational fix in (A.7) and (A.8) and the marketing hook here are the same decision. Shrink the map, go solo, and the promise becomes deliverable as a side effect of the thing you were already going to do for margin.

The offer "The same person every visit — or that clean is free."

Hard, unhedged, with a penalty attached. Nobody in this market offers it, and the penalty is what makes it credible.

Why it works commercially too 45% quality · 28% communication · 18% price

Why commercial contracts are lost. The same promise answers the top two, and neither is price.

Three more lanes standing open

1

Every local independent is quote-only. Publishing real prices from a genuinely local operator is unclaimed — and it is the same decision as settling (A.4).

2

One operator in the market so much as mentions texting. Communication is 28% of why commercial contracts are lost.

3

Pet-friendly, said out loud

Essentially unclaimed, and the plan already has pet-handling rules written. This is free.

Oak Grove and Odessa — a real tension

Zero cleaning companies are based in either town. CottageCare's service list stops at Grain Valley; everyone else dispatches 25–40 miles. That is a genuine gap — but they are underserved because they are far, which is exactly what (A.7) says destroys route density.

Best read: a surcharged outer zone served on a fixed day once the core is dense, not core launch territory. Do not let the empty map talk you into the long drive.

Do not repeat these — they did not check out

The "200–400% cleaning industry turnover" figure could not be traced to any primary source; BLS JOLTS implies roughly 55% for the sector. Household and income data for these specific towns was never obtained. grainvalleycleaning.com no longer resolves — possibly defunct, unconfirmed.

Two research threads were stopped early: mining competitor reviews for gaps by omission, and ranking underserved niches. Neither changes anything above, but neither is finished.

F

What we build instead of hire

A cleaning startup normally rents its software and hires its office. We can build most of it and run it for close to nothing — which means the seats the plan needs stay empty far longer than they otherwise would.

The headline

Roughly $2,100 a month of overhead we simply do not pay.

About $290 of that is software we build instead of rent. The other ~$1,850 is office roles the plan genuinely needs and we can leave unfilled — the weekday dispatcher, the scheduler, the person who checks the work. That is around $25,000 in the first year.

Put in the currency of this document: overhead is paid out of what is left after cleaner pay, so $2,100/mo of it is worth about 18 recurring customers you do not have to sell just to stand still.

What this does not do

It does not reduce cleaners. Cleaning is the product and their pay is 40% of revenue by design — that is the plan working, not a cost to engineer away. Every dollar above comes out of office overhead.

It also is not free in the true sense. It costs build time, and it is maintained by one person. Everything on the list above is deliberately built to keep running on its own if that person is unavailable — but new features would wait.

Why we can do this and a franchise cannot

Not because the software is clever. Because of what it is allowed to optimise for.

Rented scheduling software packs the day for maximum jobs per hour, so the time slot decides who shows up and the customer gets whoever is free. Ours can be told the opposite: keep the same cleaner on the same customer, and build the route around that. That is a one-line difference in what the software is solving for, and it is the entire promise on the previous screen (A.6).

It also means we can measure it — what share of visits were served by the assigned cleaner — and publish the number. No competitor in this market can make that claim without rebuilding how they route and how they staff. Software is how the promise stops being marketing and becomes something we can prove.

The order it gets built

Nothing here is built yet, and none of it should be built before it is needed — at three customers a spreadsheet and a group text genuinely beat any of it.
